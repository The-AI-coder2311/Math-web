summary: Learn the one idea behind many number problems. Factors come in pairs, so you can find them all without missing any.

Suppose you have 12 square tiles, and you want to arrange all of them into a rectangle. How many different rectangles can you make?

This is really a question about **factors**. A factor of a number is a whole number that divides it with nothing left over. For example, 3 is a factor of 12, because $12 \div 3 = 4$ with nothing left over.

> **The big idea:** Factors come in **pairs**. Whenever you find one factor, you automatically find its partner too.

## Example 1: Tiles make pairs

Here are the rectangles you can make with 12 tiles:

![Three rectangles made from 12 square tiles. One has 1 row of 12 tiles, one has 2 rows of 6 tiles, and one has 3 rows of 4 tiles.](/images/number-sense-tile-rectangles.svg)

Each rectangle shows one factor pair:

$$1 \times 12 = 12$$

$$2 \times 6 = 12$$

$$3 \times 4 = 12$$

So the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12. That's 3 pairs, which makes 6 factors.

## Example 2: Finding every factor of 24

Competition problems often ask how many factors a number has. To make sure you don't miss any, start at 1 and count up, writing down each pair:

- 1 works, and its partner is 24.
- 2 works, and its partner is 12.
- 3 works, and its partner is 8.
- 4 works, and its partner is 6.
- 5 doesn't work.
- 6 is already on our list, so we can **stop**.

Once you reach a number that's already on your list, you've found them all. The number 24 has 4 pairs, which makes 8 factors.

## Example 3: When a number pairs with itself

Now try 36. The pairs are 1 and 36, 2 and 18, 3 and 12, 4 and 9, and then:

$$6 \times 6 = 36$$

The number 6 is its own partner, so it only counts once. That gives 36 a total of 9 factors, which is an odd number.

Here's what to remember: **only perfect squares have an odd number of factors**, because they're the only numbers with a factor that pairs with itself. Perfect squares are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, and so on.

## Example 4: Numbers with just one pair

Some numbers have only one factor pair: 1 and the number itself. For example, the only pair for 7 is 1 and 7. These numbers are called **prime numbers**.

The first few primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, and 13. There are two surprises to watch for:

- **The number 1 is not prime.** Primes have exactly 2 factors, and 1 only has one.
- **Some numbers just look prime.** Is 51 prime? It might seem so, but $3 \times 17 = 51$, so it isn't.

## Example 5: Leftovers are factors in disguise

Omar shares 23 stickers equally among some friends, and there are 3 stickers left over. How many friends could there be?

Set aside the 3 leftover stickers. The friends shared the other stickers evenly, and $23 - 3 = 20$. So the number of friends must be a factor of 20.

The factors of 20 are 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, and 20. But there's a catch: with only 1, 2, or 3 friends, 3 leftover stickers could still be shared. So there must be more than 3 friends, which leaves 4, 5, 10, or 20.

> **Tip:** Take away the leftovers first, then look for factors. Just remember that the number of groups must be bigger than the number of leftovers.

Can you list all the factors of 15 using pairs?

The pairs are 1 and 15, and 3 and 5, so the factors are 1, 3, 5, and 15. If you got that, you're ready to practice. If not, reread Example 2, then go on. The practice problems have hints whenever you need them.
