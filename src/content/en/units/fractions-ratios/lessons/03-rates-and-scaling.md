A photo is 4 inches wide and 6 inches tall. You want to enlarge it so that it's 8 inches wide without stretching anyone's face. How tall will it be? Many people say 10 inches, since 4 more wide should mean 4 more tall. If you did that, the people in the photo would look squashed. Let's see why.

> **The big idea:** When something scales, every amount gets **multiplied** by the same number. If you aren't sure what to do, find the amount for **one**, and go from there.

## Why it's multiplying and not adding

Let's take the photo and draw 2 copies of it, side by side and stacked up. Doubling the width and doubling the height gives a picture with the same shape. A 4 by 6 photo becomes 8 by 12. So the height should be 12, not 10.

Now test the wrong way with a very small photo. A 1 by 100 bookmark, grown by "adding 9 to each side," becomes 10 by 109. It was a long, thin strip, and now it's only about 11 times as long as it is wide, instead of 100 times. It's a different shape. Adding changes shapes, and multiplying keeps them.

A **rate** is a comparison with a "per" in it: dollars per notebook, miles per hour, or words per minute. It's the amount for one.

## Example 1: Finding the amount for one

Three notebooks cost \$4.50. How much do 7 notebooks cost?

**What do we know?** We know the cost of 3, and we want the cost of 7. Since 7 isn't a nice multiple of 3, let's go through 1.

**The amount for one:** $4.50 \div 3 = 1.50$, so one notebook costs \$1.50.

**Scale up:** $7 \times 1.50 = 10.50$, so 7 notebooks cost \$10.50.

Try one. If 4 bags of chips cost \$6, how much do 10 bags cost?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

One bag costs $6 \div 4 = 1.50$ dollars, so 10 bags cost $10 \times 1.50 = 15$ dollars.

</details>

## Example 2: A recipe

A recipe that serves 6 people uses 4 cups of flour. How much flour do you need for 15 people?

**Is there a shortcut?** You could find the amount for one person, which is $\frac{4}{6}$ of a cup, but that's a messy fraction. Instead, use a friendlier scale. Since $15 \div 6 = 2.5$, we're making 2.5 times the recipe:

$$4 \times 2.5 = 10$$

So you need 10 cups. You can also see this by building up: 6 people use 4 cups, 12 people use 8 cups, and 3 more people (half of 6) use 2 more cups. That also gives 10.

## Example 3: Comparing rates

Store A sells 8 pencils for \$2. Store B sells 12 pencils for \$2.40. Which is the better deal?

**What's the question really asking?** Which price per pencil is lower?

- Store A: $2 \div 8 = 0.25$, so it's 25 cents each.
- Store B: $2.40 \div 12 = 0.20$, so it's 20 cents each.

Store B is cheaper per pencil, even though its total is higher. Comparing totals wouldn't work, since the amounts are different.

## Example 4: Units along the way

A snail moves 3 feet in 2 minutes. How many inches does it move in 1 hour, at the same speed?

**What do we know?** There's a mismatch: the question asks for inches and hours, but we know feet and minutes. Convert one step at a time, and keep track of the labels.

- Per minute, the snail moves $3 \div 2 = 1.5$ feet.
- In 1 hour, which is 60 minutes, it moves $60 \times 1.5 = 90$ feet.
- Each foot is 12 inches, so it moves $90 \times 12 = 1080$ inches.

## Example 5: A contest-style question

Three painters can paint 2 identical rooms in 4 hours. Working at the same rate, how many hours would 6 painters take to paint 8 of these rooms?

**What do we know?** More painters makes the job faster, and more rooms makes it slower.

**What's the amount for one?** Let's use "painter-hours," which is the number of painters times the number of hours. Three painters for 4 hours is $3 \times 4 = 12$ painter-hours, and that gets 2 rooms done. So one room takes $12 \div 2 = 6$ painter-hours.

**Scale up:** 8 rooms need $8 \times 6 = 48$ painter-hours. With 6 painters, that takes $48 \div 6 = 8$ hours.

## Going deeper

Rates with several parts can fool your intuition. The fix is to find the rate for **one** worker in **one** unit of time.

**A harder one:** Three cats catch 3 mice in 3 minutes. At that rate, how many cats does it take to catch 100 mice in 100 minutes?

The tempting answer is 100. Let's find the rate for one cat.

**What does one cat do?** Three cats catch 3 mice in 3 minutes, so one cat catches 1 mouse in 3 minutes.

**How much can one cat do in 100 minutes?** It catches $\frac{100}{3}$ mice.

**How many cats do we need?** For 100 mice, it's $100 \div \frac{100}{3} = 3$ cats.

The answer is still 3! More time alone lets the same 3 cats do more. When an answer seems to come from a pattern in the numbers, stop and find the rate for one.

Try this. Five machines make 5 toys in 5 minutes. How long do 100 machines take to make 100 toys?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

One machine makes 1 toy in 5 minutes. With 100 machines, each makes its one toy in those same 5 minutes. So it takes 5 minutes.

</details>

<section data-box="traps">

## Common traps

**Adding instead of multiplying.** The most common mistake is the one from the opening: going from 4 to 8 and saying "add 4 to everything." Ask yourself, "How many **times** bigger did it get?"

**Assuming that more means more.** If 2 people take 6 hours to mow a field, then 4 people take 3 hours, not 12. More workers means **less** time. Before you scale, ask whether the answer should go up or down.

**Ignoring units.** If one speed is in feet per minute and the question asks about hours, your answer can be off by a factor of 60. Write the units next to your numbers, and check that they match the question.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Look for "per," "at this rate," "at the same speed," or "how many would it take."

**Pick your path:** If the numbers divide nicely, go through "the amount for one." If not, look for a friendly multiple, as in Example 2.

**Check the direction:** Before you calculate, decide whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than the number you started with. This alone can eliminate half of the answer choices.

**Time:** About a minute for one step, or about two for problems with several steps.

- Scaling means multiplying, not adding.

- Compare rates by finding the price or amount per one.
