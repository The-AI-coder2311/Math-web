A pitcher of punch uses 2 cups of juice for every 3 cups of water. You want 20 cups of punch for a party. How much juice do you need? Plenty of people want to set up a complicated equation, but you won't need one. You'll need a pencil and some boxes.

> **The big idea:** A ratio like 2 to 3 means the amounts come in **equal-sized groups**: 2 groups of one thing for every 3 groups of another. Draw a box for each group, and work out what goes in one box.

## Why boxes work

The ratio 2 to 3 doesn't mean that there are exactly 2 and 3 of anything. It means that however much juice is in a "scoop," you add the same scoop of water for each scoop of juice. Every box holds the same amount, so once you know what's in one box, you know everything.

![Five equal boxes. Two boxes of juice hold 4 cups each, making 8 cups. Three boxes of water hold 4 cups each, making 12 cups.](/images/fractions-ratio-boxes.svg)

There are 5 boxes and 20 cups in all, so each box holds $20 \div 5 = 4$ cups. That means $2 \times 4 = 8$ cups of juice and $3 \times 4 = 12$ cups of water. Check: $8 + 12 = 20$.

Let's test the idea with a tiny case. With only 5 cups of punch, each box would hold 1 cup, so we'd have 2 cups of juice and 3 cups of water. That's exactly the recipe.

## Example 1: You know the total

A bag has red and blue marbles in a ratio of 3 to 7, and there are 40 marbles in all. How many are blue?

**What do we know?** The total is 40, and there are $3 + 7 = 10$ boxes.

**What goes in one box?** $40 \div 10 = 4$ marbles.

**What's the question asking?** Blue fills 7 boxes, so there are $7 \times 4 = 28$ blue marbles.

Try one yourself. A class has boys and girls in a ratio of 4 to 5, with 36 students in all. How many girls are there?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

There are 9 boxes, so each box holds $36 \div 9 = 4$ students. Girls fill 5 boxes: $5 \times 4 = 20$.

</details>

## Example 2: You know one part

The ratio of cats to dogs at a shelter is 5 to 2, and there are 14 dogs. How many cats are there?

**What's different?** We know the number in the dog boxes, not the total. Two dog boxes hold 14 dogs, so one box holds $14 \div 2 = 7$ animals. Five cat boxes hold $5 \times 7 = 35$ cats.

## Example 3: You know the difference

Sam and Rae share some stickers in a ratio of 7 to 4. Sam gets 18 more stickers than Rae. How many stickers are there in all?

**What do we know?** Sam has $7 - 4 = 3$ more boxes than Rae. Those 3 extra boxes hold the 18 extra stickers.

**What goes in one box?** $18 \div 3 = 6$ stickers.

**What's the question asking?** There are 11 boxes in all, and $11 \times 6 = 66$.

Notice that all three examples ask the same thing: "What's in one box?" The only change is which fact gets you there.

## Example 4: Linking two ratios

The ratio of apples to oranges is 2 to 3, and the ratio of oranges to pears is 4 to 5. What's the ratio of apples to pears?

**What's the problem?** Oranges appear in both ratios, but they're worth 3 in one and 4 in the other, so the box sizes don't match.

**How can we fix it?** Rewrite both ratios so that the oranges match. A ratio stays the same if you multiply both parts by the same number. A number that works for both 3 and 4 is 12:

| | Apples | Oranges | Pears |
|---|---|---|---|
| First ratio, times 4 | 8 | 12 | |
| Second ratio, times 3 | | 12 | 15 |

So apples to pears is 8 to 15.

## Example 5: A contest-style question

A bowl holds red and green candies in a ratio of 3 to 5. After 8 red candies are added, the ratio becomes 1 to 1. How many candies were in the bowl to start?

**What do we know?** At the start, red has 3 boxes and green has 5. To make the amounts equal, red needs 2 more boxes. Adding 8 candies did exactly that.

**What goes in one box?** $8 \div 2 = 4$ candies.

**What's the question asking?** The start had 8 boxes, so there were $8 \times 4 = 32$ candies.

**Check:** At the start, there were 12 red and 20 green candies. After adding 8 red, there are 20 of each.

## Going deeper

When both amounts change by the same number, their **difference** stays the same. That's a fixed point you can use to line up two ratios.

**A harder one:** The ages of Ty and his sister are in a ratio of 3 to 5. In 6 years, the ratio will be 2 to 3. How old are they now?

**What stays the same?** The difference between their ages. Now it's 2 boxes, since $5 - 3 = 2$. In 6 years, it's 1 box of a different size, since $3 - 2 = 1$.

**Make the differences match.** Double the second ratio, so that its difference is also 2: 2 to 3 becomes 4 to 6. Now the boxes are the same size:

| | Ty | Sister |
|---|---|---|
| Now | 3 boxes | 5 boxes |
| In 6 years | 4 boxes | 6 boxes |

Each person gains 1 box, and that box is 6 years. So Ty is $3 \times 6 = 18$, and his sister is $5 \times 6 = 30$.

**Check:** In 6 years, they'll be 24 and 36, and 24 to 36 is 2 to 3.

Try this. Two ages are in a ratio of 4 to 7. In 3 years, the ratio will be 5 to 8. How old is the older person now?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

Both differences are 3 boxes, so the boxes already match. Each person gains 1 box, which is 3 years. So the older person is $7 \times 3 = 21$.

</details>

## Common traps

**Confusing part-to-part with part-to-whole.** If juice to water is 2 to 3, juice is **not** $\frac{2}{3}$ of the punch. It's 2 boxes out of 5, so it's $\frac{2}{5}$. A ratio compares two parts, and a fraction compares a part to the whole.

**Treating ratio numbers as the actual amounts.** Suppose the ratio of red to blue is 3 to 7, and there are 40 marbles. Answering "7 are blue" ignores the total. The 7 counts boxes, not marbles.

**Adding to both parts to "keep" a ratio.** Is 2 to 3 the same as 4 to 5? You added 2 to both, but they aren't equal. For example, $\frac{2}{3}$ is about 0.67, and $\frac{4}{5}$ is 0.8. To keep a ratio, you have to **multiply** both parts by the same number.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Look for "for every," "ratio," "in the ratio," or "A to B."

**The answer has to fit the boxes:** If red and green candies are in a ratio of 3 to 5, the total must be a multiple of 8. Any answer choice that isn't a multiple of 8 can be crossed out. That can solve the problem before you do any math.

**How long it should take:** Draw the boxes first, even in your head. Most of these take under two minutes.

<section data-box="summary">

## Remember this

- Draw a box for each part of the ratio.
- Ask, "What's in one box?"
- A ratio compares part to part. A fraction compares part to whole.
- To keep a ratio, multiply both parts. Don't add.
- To link two ratios, make the shared part match.
