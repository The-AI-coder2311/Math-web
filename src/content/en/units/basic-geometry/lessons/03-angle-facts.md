Draw any triangle you like on a sheet of paper: tall, wide, or lopsided. Tear off its three corners, and fit them together with the points touching. They'll make a perfectly straight line, every time. Why would that be true for every triangle? It seems too neat to be real, and it's one of the most useful facts in geometry.

> **The big idea:** Three facts solve most angle problems. Angles on a **straight line** add up to 180°, angles **around a point** add up to 360°, and the angles of **any triangle** add up to 180°.

![Three angle facts. Angles on a straight line, 130 and 50 degrees, add up to 180. Angles around a point, 120, 150 and 90 degrees, add up to 360. The angles of a triangle, 50, 60 and 70 degrees, add up to 180.](/images/geometry-angle-facts.svg)

## Why the facts are true

An angle measures how much something turns, in **degrees**. A full spin is 360°. That's the fact about going around a point: if you stand at the point and turn through all the angles, you've made one full spin. A straight line is a half turn, so it's 180°. A square corner, or **right angle**, is a quarter turn, which is 90°.

Why does a triangle add up to 180°? Imagine walking around a triangle with your pencil pointing forward. At each corner, you slide the pencil around that inside angle, flipping it to point the other way. After the three corners, the pencil has made a half turn. So all three angles together make a half turn, which is 180°. That's what your torn-off corners showed too.

## Example 1: Finding a missing angle

Two angles of a triangle are 47° and 68°. What's the third?

**What do we know?** All three add up to 180°.

$$180 - 47 - 68 = 65$$

So the third angle is 65°. Next, try a straight-line one. Two angles sit side by side on a straight line, and one is 115°. What's the other?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

Angles on a straight line add up to 180°, so the other is $180 - 115 = 65$ degrees.

</details>

## Example 2: Isosceles triangles

An **isosceles** triangle has two equal sides, and the angles across from those sides are equal too. Picture folding it down the middle: the two sides match, so the two angles must match as well.

The top angle of an isosceles triangle is 40°. What are the other two?

**What do we know?** The other two are equal, and they share what's left. They share $180 - 40 = 140$ degrees, so each one is $140 \div 2 = 70$ degrees.

Another special case: an **equilateral** triangle has three equal sides, so it has three equal angles. Each one is $180 \div 3 = 60$ degrees.

## Example 3: Angles and letters

Three angles around a point measure $x$, $2x$, and $3x$ degrees. What's the largest?

**What do we know?** They add up to 360°, and together they make $6x$ in all. This is the same as the boxes you used for ratios: 1 box, 2 boxes, and 3 boxes make 6 boxes.

**What's in one box?** $360 \div 6 = 60$. So the largest angle is $3 \times 60 = 180$ degrees.

**Check:** $60 + 120 + 180 = 360$. It works.

## Example 4: Step by step through a diagram

Triangle $ABC$ has angle $A$ equal to 50° and angle $B$ equal to 60°. Side $BC$ is extended past $C$ to a point $D$, making a straight line. What's angle $ACD$?

**Draw it.** This is the key step. Sketch the triangle, and extend the side past $C$.

**What can we find?** Find whatever we can, even if it isn't what's being asked.

- Angle $ACB$ is $180 - 50 - 60 = 70$ degrees.
- Angles $ACB$ and $ACD$ are on a straight line, so angle $ACD$ is $180 - 70 = 110$ degrees.

Did you notice that $110 = 50 + 60$? That's always the case: an outside angle equals the sum of the two far inside angles. You'll use this in Level 2.

## Example 5: A contest-style question

In triangle $PQR$, sides $PQ$ and $PR$ are equal, and angle $P$ is 30° more than angle $Q$. What is angle $P$?

**What do we know?** Since $PQ$ and $PR$ are equal, the angles across from them, $R$ and $Q$, are equal. Let's call each of them $y$.

**What's the question really asking?** Angle $P$ is $y + 30$. All three add up to 180°:

$$y + y + y + 30 = 180$$

So 3 copies of $y$ make 150, which means each one is $150 \div 3 = 50$ degrees. Then angle $P$ is $50 + 30 = 80$ degrees.

**Check:** $50 + 50 + 80 = 180$. It works. Notice that the first thing we did was work out which angles are equal. On problems like this, that's usually the key step.

## Going deeper

Angles also show up where you might not expect them, such as on a clock.

**A harder one:** What's the angle between the hands of a clock at 3:30?

**What do we know?** A full circle is $360^\circ$, and the clock has 12 hours, so each hour mark is $30^\circ$ from the next.

**Where's the minute hand?** At 30 minutes, it points straight down at the 6.

**Where's the hour hand?** Here's the trap: it isn't on the 3. At 3:30, the hour hand is halfway from the 3 to the 4.

**What's the gap?** From the 3 to the 6 is 3 hour marks, or $90^\circ$. The hour hand has moved halfway to the 4, which is $15^\circ$, closing the gap. So the angle is $90 - 15 = 75$ degrees.

**Another way to think about it:** The hour hand moves $30^\circ$ in 60 minutes, which is half a degree per minute. In 30 minutes, it moves $15^\circ$.

Try this. What's the angle between the hands at 2:30?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

From the 2 to the 6 is 4 hour marks, or $120^\circ$. The hour hand has moved $15^\circ$ toward the 3. So the angle is $120 - 15 = 105$ degrees.

</details>

<section data-box="traps">

## Common traps

**Assuming that a picture shows the truth.** An angle that looks like 90° might not be. Only trust a right angle if it's marked or stated. Go by the numbers, not by how it looks.

**Matching the wrong equal angles.** The equal angles in an isosceles triangle are **across from** the equal sides. In Example 5, sides $PQ$ and $PR$ meet at $P$, so the equal angles are $Q$ and $R$, not $P$ and $Q$.

**Answering with a step, not the question.** In Example 4, 70° is a useful step, but the question asks for 110°. Both could be answer choices. Reread the question before you choose.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Any diagram with degree marks, or the words "angle," "isosceles," or "equilateral."

**Fill in everything.** Write each angle you find on the diagram, even if you don't yet know how it helps. Often the answer appears on its own.

**Time:** About a minute per step. If you're stuck, look for a triangle in which you know two angles, or a straight line in which you know one.

**Estimating:** If a figure is drawn to scale, you can often eliminate choices by eye. A sharp, narrow angle can't be 110°.

## Remember this

- Straight line: 180°. Around a point: 360°. Triangle: 180°.
- Isosceles: the two angles across from the equal sides are equal.
- Equilateral: all three angles are 60°.
- Write every angle you find on the diagram.
- Don't trust how a picture looks. Go by the numbers.
