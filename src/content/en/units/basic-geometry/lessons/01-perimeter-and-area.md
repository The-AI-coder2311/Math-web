Sam has 12 meters of fence for a rectangular garden. Does it matter what shape the rectangle is? After all, it's the same amount of fence, so maybe the garden comes out the same size either way. Take a guess, and then let's find out.

Good geometry problem solvers aren't people who memorized the most formulas. They're people who **draw a picture first**, and that's the habit you'll build in this unit.

> **The big idea:** **Perimeter** is the distance around a shape, like a fence. **Area** is the space inside, like the grass that the fence surrounds. They measure different things, so knowing one doesn't tell you the other.

## Why the formulas work

Take a rectangle that's 5 meters long and 3 meters wide.

To find its **perimeter**, walk all the way around it and add up the sides: $5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16$ meters. Each side length appears twice, so a shortcut is to add the length and width, and double that.

To find its **area**, count how many 1-meter squares fit inside. There are 3 rows, and each row has 5 squares. That's why we multiply: $3 \times 5 = 15$ square meters. Multiplying isn't only a rule to remember. It's a fast way to count squares in rows.

Notice the units. Perimeter is a length, so it's measured in meters. Area counts squares, so it's measured in **square** meters. If a problem asks for an area, the answer can't be in plain meters.

## Example 1: The surprise about rectangles

Back to Sam's 12 meters of fence. Half of the fence covers one length and one width, so the length and width must add up to 6. Let's list every possibility using whole numbers.

![Three rectangles that each have perimeter 12. The 1 by 5 rectangle has area 5, the 2 by 4 rectangle has area 8, and the 3 by 3 square has area 9.](/images/geometry-same-perimeter.svg)

They use the same fence, but the areas are 5, 8, and 9. So shape matters a lot! As the rectangle gets closer to a square, the area grows. For a fixed perimeter, a square gives the most area.

The reverse surprise is true as well. Two rectangles with the same area can have different perimeters. A 1 by 12 rectangle and a 3 by 4 rectangle both have an area of 12, but their perimeters are 26 and 14.

Now try this one. A rectangle is 7 by 4. What are its perimeter and its area?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

The perimeter is $7 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 22$. The area is $7 \times 4 = 28$. Those are different numbers measuring different things, in different units.

</details>

## Example 2: Going backwards

A square has an area of 49 square centimeters. What's its perimeter?

**What do we know?** The area of a square is its side times itself. Which number times itself gives 49? It's 7, so each side is 7 centimeters.

**What's the question asking?** There are 4 equal sides, so the perimeter is $4 \times 7 = 28$ centimeters.

Going from area to side length is a move you'll use often. It helps to know the perfect squares up to about $15 \times 15 = 225$.

## Example 3: Finding a missing side

A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 and a length of 9. What's its area?

**What do we know?** Sketch the rectangle, label the length 9, and label the unknown width $w$.

**What's a smaller step?** Half of the perimeter is one length plus one width. So $9 + w = 15$, and the width is 6.

**What's the question asking?** The area is $9 \times 6 = 54$.

## Example 4: A contest-style question

A rectangular patio is made of square tiles, each 1 foot on a side. It's 6 tiles wide and 10 tiles long. A border of tiles, one tile thick, is added all the way around. How many tiles are in the border?

**Draw it!** The patio with its border forms a bigger rectangle. Because the border adds a tile on **both** ends, the bigger rectangle is 8 by 12.

**What's the plan?** Find the border by subtracting the inside from the whole:

$$8 \times 12 - 6 \times 10 = 96 - 60 = 36$$

There's a common wrong answer here. The patio's perimeter is 32, so some students say 32 tiles. But the border also needs tiles at the 4 corners, which don't touch any side of the patio. And $32 + 4 = 36$. It matches.

## Common traps

**Mixing up the formulas.** If you add for area or multiply for perimeter, you'll get a number that looks fine but means nothing. Ask yourself, "Am I walking around it, or filling it with squares?"

**Forgetting half of the sides.** For a 5 by 3 rectangle, the answer $5 + 3 = 8$ is only half of the perimeter. Every side appears twice. The perimeter is 16.

**Assuming that same area means same perimeter.** Neither one decides the other, as the 1 by 12 and 3 by 4 rectangles show.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Look for the words "fence," "border," "tiles," "around," or "covers."

**Always draw and label.** Even a rough sketch prevents most mistakes. It takes 10 seconds, and it often shows you the next step.

**Time:** About a minute for the basic questions. For a border question, draw a small version first.

**Using the answer choices:** The maximum area for a given perimeter comes from the shape closest to a square, so you can often jump directly to that case.

<section data-box="summary">

## Remember this

- Perimeter means around. Area means inside.
- Area counts squares, so its units are square units.
- A rectangle's length plus width is half of its perimeter.
- Same perimeter doesn't mean same area.
- For a fixed perimeter, a square has the most area.
