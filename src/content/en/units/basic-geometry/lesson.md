Good geometry problem solvers aren't people who memorized the most formulas. They're people who **draw a picture first**. This lesson gives you the most useful facts about shapes, along with the reasons they're true.

> **The big idea:** Before you calculate anything, draw the shape and label everything you know. Most of the problem often solves itself after that.

## Example 1: Perimeter and area are different questions

**Perimeter** is the distance around the outside of a shape, like a fence. **Area** is the space inside, like the grass the fence surrounds.

Take a rectangle that's 5 meters long and 3 meters wide:

- To find its perimeter, walk all the way around: $5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16$ meters.
- To find its area, count the squares that fill it. There are 3 rows of 5 squares: $3 \times 5 = 15$ square meters.

Perimeter is measured in regular units, like meters. Area is measured in **square units**, like square meters, because it counts squares.

## Example 2: A surprise about rectangles

If two rectangles have the same perimeter, do they have the same area? Take a guess, and then look at these three rectangles. Each one has a perimeter of 12:

![Three rectangles that each have perimeter 12. The 1 by 5 rectangle has area 5, the 2 by 4 rectangle has area 8, and the 3 by 3 square has area 9.](/images/geometry-same-perimeter.svg)

They have the same perimeter but different areas! So knowing one doesn't tell you the other. Also notice that the area grows as the rectangle gets closer to a square. For a fixed perimeter, a square gives the most area.

## Example 3: Why a triangle's area has a one half

Draw a rectangle around a triangle, using the triangle's base and height:

![A triangle with base 8 and height 5 inside a rectangle that is 8 by 5. The triangle covers exactly half of the rectangle.](/images/geometry-triangle-half-rectangle.svg)

The dashed height line cuts the rectangle into two smaller rectangles. The triangle's side cuts each of those in half, so the triangle covers exactly half of the big rectangle. That's where the formula comes from:

$$\text{Area of a triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$$

For this triangle, the area is $\frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 5 = 20$.

> **Watch out:** The height must meet the base at a **right angle**, like a wall meeting a floor. A slanted side usually isn't the height.

## Example 4: Three angle facts

Angles are measured in degrees. These three facts solve a lot of problems:

- Angles that make a **straight line** add up to 180°.
- Angles all the way **around a point** add up to 360°.
- The three angles of **any triangle** add up to 180°.

You can test the last fact yourself. Draw any triangle on paper, tear off the three corners, and fit them together. They'll make a straight line.

Here's how to use it. In an **isosceles** triangle, two sides are equal, and the two angles across from those sides are equal too. Suppose the top angle is 40°. The other two angles share what's left, since $180 - 40 = 140$. They're equal, so each is $140 \div 2 = 70$ degrees.

## Example 5: Breaking up a shape

A rectangle that's 8 by 6 has a small 3 by 2 rectangle cut out of one corner, making an L shape. What's its area?

Think of the big rectangle minus the missing piece: $8 \times 6 = 48$ and $3 \times 2 = 6$, so the area is $48 - 6 = 42$.

What about its perimeter? You might expect it to change, but picture sliding the two sides of the notch outward until the corner is square again. They fill the gaps exactly. So the L shape has the same perimeter as the full rectangle: $8 + 6 + 8 + 6 = 28$.

This also shows why you can't work out perimeter from area. The cut made the area smaller, but the perimeter stayed the same.

## Example 6: Right triangles

A **right triangle** has one 90° angle. Its longest side is across from that angle and is called the **hypotenuse**. The other two sides are called **legs**. The Pythagorean Theorem connects them:

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

Here, $a$ and $b$ are the legs and $c$ is the hypotenuse. The most famous example has sides 3, 4, and 5, since $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, and $5^2 = 25$.

Suppose you walk 6 blocks east and then 8 blocks north. How far are you from where you started, in a straight line? Your path makes a right angle, so draw the triangle. Then:

$$6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$$

Which number squared is 100? It's 10, so you're 10 blocks away.

> **Tip:** Contest writers love whole-number right triangles. Remember 3-4-5, 5-12-13, and 8-15-17. Multiples work too, such as 6-8-10.

## Your turn

Before you start the practice problems, try these two:

1. What are the perimeter and area of a rectangle that's 7 by 4?
2. Two angles of a triangle are 90° and 35°. What's the third angle?

**Answers:** For the first, the perimeter is $7 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 22$ and the area is $7 \times 4 = 28$. For the second, $180 - 90 - 35 = 55$, so the third angle is 55°.

If you got those, you're ready. If not, reread Examples 1 and 4, then go on. Remember to draw a picture for every practice problem.
