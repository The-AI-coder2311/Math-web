Tickets cost \$6 for adults and \$4 for students. A show sold 50 tickets for a total of \$230. How many adult tickets were sold? There are two unknowns here: adults and students. But one of them can be written using the other. If you know how many adult tickets were sold, you immediately know how many student tickets were sold.

> **The big idea:** With two unknowns, you need two facts. Use one fact to **rewrite** one unknown in terms of the other, or **add or subtract** the equations to make one unknown disappear.

## Why you need two facts

Suppose all you know is that two numbers add up to 10. There are lots of possibilities: 1 and 9, 2 and 8, 3 and 7, and so on. A second fact, such as "they differ by 4," narrows it down to only one pair, 7 and 3. Each fact cuts down the possibilities until one is left. A pair of equations like this is called a **system**.

## Method 1: Rewrite one unknown

Here's the ticket problem. Let $a$ be the number of adult tickets. The rest are student tickets, so there are $50 - a$. Adding up the money gives:

$$6a + 4(50 - a) = 230$$

Multiply out to get $6a + 200 - 4a = 230$. That simplifies to $2a + 200 = 230$, so $2a = 30$ and $a = 15$.

**Check:** 15 adult tickets bring in \$90. The other 35 are student tickets, which bring in \$140. Together, that's \$230.

## A clever way to see it

Here's another way to think about the same problem. Imagine that all 50 tickets were student tickets. That would bring in $50 \times 4 = 200$ dollars. But the show made \$30 more than that. Each adult ticket adds \$2 more than a student ticket. So there must be $30 \div 2 = 15$ adult tickets. It's the same arithmetic as the equation! This is the **"pretend they're all the same"** method, and it's great for mental math.

## Method 2: Add or subtract the equations

Two numbers have a sum of 26 and a difference of 8. What are they?

**What do we know?** Let the numbers be $x$ and $y$, so that $x + y = 26$ and $x - y = 8$.

**What's the plan?** If we add the two equations together, then the $y$ and $-y$ cancel out.

$$2x = 34$$

So $x = 17$. Then $y = 26 - 17 = 9$.

**A shortcut:** The larger number is the average of the sum and the difference, $(26 + 8) \div 2$, and the smaller is $(26 - 8) \div 2$.

Now you try. Two numbers add up to 40, and their difference is 12. What's the larger number?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

Adding the equations gives $2x = 52$, so $x = 26$. The other number is 14. Check: $26 + 14 = 40$, and $26 - 14 = 12$.

</details>

## Example 1: Pencils and erasers

Two pencils and 3 erasers cost \$1.30. Two pencils and 1 eraser cost 90 cents. How much is one eraser?

**What do we know?** Working in cents, $2p + 3e = 130$ and $2p + e = 90$.

**What's the plan?** Look at what's different between the two shopping lists. The first has 2 more erasers, and it costs 40 cents more. So 2 erasers cost 40 cents, and one costs 20 cents. Subtracting the equations is the same as comparing the two lists.

## Example 2: Making amounts match

A farmer has chickens and cows. In total, there are 20 heads and 56 legs. How many cows are there?

**Pretend they're all the same.** If all 20 animals were chickens, there would be 40 legs. We have 16 extra legs. Each cow has 2 more legs than a chicken, so there are $16 \div 2 = 8$ cows.

**With equations:** $c + d = 20$ and $2c + 4d = 56$, where $c$ is chickens and $d$ is cows. Multiply the first equation by 2 to get $2c + 2d = 40$. Subtract that from the second to get $2d = 16$, so $d = 8$. We multiplied first, to make the $c$ terms match, and then subtracted.

## Example 3: A contest-style question

An apple and a banana together weigh 11 ounces. A banana and a cherry cup together weigh 15 ounces. An apple and a cherry cup together weigh 14 ounces. How much do all three weigh together?

**What do we know?** There are three unknowns, and it looks like a lot of work: $a + b = 11$, $b + c = 15$, and $a + c = 14$.

**What's the question really asking?** It's about the total, $a + b + c$, not any single item. Look at what happens when you add all three equations together. Each item appears exactly twice:

$$2a + 2b + 2c = 40$$

So $a + b + c = 20$. We never needed to find the weight of any single item. When a question asks for a combination, look for a way to get it directly, before you solve for each letter.

<section data-box="traps">

## Common traps

**Finding the wrong unknown.** In the ticket problem, you might let $a$ be adults, then find 15, and then the question might ask for student tickets. That's 35. Test writers usually include the other number as a choice.

**Adding when you need to multiply first.** In the chickens and cows problem, adding them right away gives $3c + 5d = 76$, which doesn't help. An unknown only disappears if its amounts match, so multiply an equation first.

**Sign errors when subtracting.** When you subtract, $x - (-y)$ becomes $x + y$. Writing out each step helps avoid this mistake.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Look for two kinds of things and a total of each kind, such as heads and legs, or tickets and money.

**Fastest path:** Try "pretend they're all the same" first. It usually takes under a minute, and it needs no algebra.

**Look before solving:** If the question asks for a sum or some other combination, you may be able to skip finding each letter.

**Time:** Two minutes. Test your answer in both of the original facts, since it has to fit both.
