Mia can paint a fence in 3 hours. Jo can paint the same fence in 6 hours. How long does it take if they work together? Lots of people say 9 hours, because $3 + 6 = 9$. But that can't be right. With two people working, the job should take **less** time than Mia needs alone, not more! Others guess the average, 4.5 hours, and that's also too long, for the same reason.

> **The big idea:** Don't add the **times**. Add the **rates**. Ask how much of the job each person gets done in **one hour**.

## Why rates add and times don't

A rate is how much gets done per hour. If Mia finishes the fence in 3 hours, then she paints $\frac{1}{3}$ of the fence each hour. If Jo finishes in 6 hours, then Jo paints $\frac{1}{6}$ of it each hour. In one hour together, they do both of those amounts:

$$\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$

They paint half of the fence per hour, so the whole fence takes 2 hours.

Let's make sure this answer passes a common-sense test. It's less than 3 hours, which is what Mia needs alone, so it's reasonable. You can also use a tiny example: if 2 identical workers each take 4 hours, then together they should take 2 hours. The rates are $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$, which gives 2 hours. It works.

## Example 1: A pretend job size

Here's a way to avoid fractions. Pretend the fence has some number of boards that both 3 and 6 divide evenly. Let's use 6 boards.

- Mia paints 2 boards per hour, since she finishes 6 in 3 hours.
- Jo paints 1 board per hour, since Jo finishes 6 in 6 hours.
- Together, they paint 3 boards per hour.

So 6 boards take $6 \div 3 = 2$ hours. The actual size of the job doesn't matter, so you can choose whatever number makes the math friendly.

Try one. One hose fills a pool in 4 hours, and another fills it in 12 hours. How long do they take together?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

Pretend the pool holds 12 buckets. The first hose fills 3 buckets per hour, and the second fills 1 bucket per hour. Together, they fill 4 buckets per hour, so 12 buckets take $12 \div 4 = 3$ hours.

</details>

## Example 2: One helps, then the other

A printer can finish a job in 10 hours, and a faster printer can do it in 5 hours. The slower printer runs alone for 4 hours, and then the faster one joins it. How much longer does the job take?

**What do we know?** Pretend the job has 10 pages. The slow printer does 1 page per hour, and the fast printer does 2 pages per hour.

**What's the plan?** Follow the story in order.

- After 4 hours alone, the slow printer has done 4 pages, leaving 6 pages.
- Together, they do 3 pages per hour.
- The last 6 pages take $6 \div 3 = 2$ more hours.

## Example 3: Something working against you

A sink fills in 6 minutes with the drain closed. With the faucet off, a full sink empties in 10 minutes. If the faucet is on and the drain is open, how long does it take to fill the sink?

**What do we know?** Now the rates pull in opposite directions, so we subtract. Let the sink hold 30 cups, since both 6 and 10 divide 30.

- The faucet adds 5 cups per minute.
- The drain removes 3 cups per minute.
- The sink gains $5 - 3 = 2$ cups per minute.

So it fills in $30 \div 2 = 15$ minutes.

## Example 4: Finding one worker's time

Ana and Ben together can mow a lawn in 2 hours. Ana alone takes 3 hours. How long does Ben take alone?

**What do we know?** Together, they do $\frac{1}{2}$ of the lawn per hour, and Ana does $\frac{1}{3}$ per hour.

**What's Ben's rate?** Subtract what Ana does from the total:

$$\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{6} - \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{6}$$

Ben does $\frac{1}{6}$ of the lawn per hour, so he takes 6 hours alone.

## Example 5: A contest-style question

Three workers, each working at the same rate, can build 4 identical tables in 6 days. How many days would it take 2 of these workers to build 6 tables?

**What's the amount for one?** Let's count **worker-days**, which is workers times days. Three workers for 6 days make 18 worker-days, and that builds 4 tables. So each table takes $18 \div 4 = 4.5$ worker-days.

**What's the question asking?** Six tables take $6 \times 4.5 = 27$ worker-days. With 2 workers, that's $27 \div 2 = 13.5$ days.

**Does that make sense?** There are fewer workers and more tables, so it should take much longer than 6 days. It does.

<section data-box="traps">

## Common traps

**Adding the times.** Saying that 3 hours and 6 hours make 9 hours together suggests that a helper makes the job take longer. Together must always be **faster** than the fastest person alone.

**Averaging the times.** The average of 3 and 6 is 4.5, but that's longer than Mia alone. If the average were right, adding a helper would slow Mia down!

**Stopping at the rate.** After finding $\frac{1}{2}$ of the job per hour, some students answer $\frac{1}{2}$ hour. A rate of $\frac{1}{2}$ per hour means the job takes 2 hours. The time is the rate flipped over.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Look for "together," "working at the same rate," pipes, painters, or printers.

**Choose a friendly job size:** Pick a number that all of the times divide into. It removes the fractions completely.

**Eliminate:** The answer for two people working together must be less than the faster person's time. On a multiple-choice test, this often rules out two or three choices.

**Time:** About a minute and a half.

</section>

<section data-box="summary">

## Remember this

- Add rates, not times.
- Rate = 1 job ÷ time.
- Pick a friendly job size to avoid fractions.
- Working against you? Subtract that rate.
- Together is always faster than the fastest person alone.
