You drive 60 miles to a city at 60 miles per hour, and you drive back at 30 miles per hour. What's your average speed for the whole trip? Nearly everyone says 45, since that's halfway between 60 and 30. That's wrong, and by the end of this lesson, you'll be able to explain why.

> **The big idea:** Distance, speed, and time are connected by one formula: **distance = speed × time**. And average speed is always **total distance divided by total time**.

## Why the formula works

If you walk at 3 miles per hour, you cover 3 miles in each hour. So in 2 hours, you've covered 2 groups of 3 miles, or 6 miles. That's all that the formula says. You can rearrange it to find whichever piece is missing:

| To find | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | speed × time | 12 mph for 3 hours is 36 miles |
| Speed | distance ÷ time | 36 miles in 3 hours is 12 mph |
| Time | distance ÷ speed | 36 miles at 12 mph is 3 hours |

The units must match. If the speed is in miles per **hour**, the time has to be in **hours**. For example, 20 minutes is $\frac{1}{3}$ of an hour, and not 0.2 of an hour.

## Example 1: Changing units

A bike rider travels at 18 miles per hour for 40 minutes. How far does the rider go?

**What do we know?** The speed is per hour, but the time is in minutes.

**What's the plan?** Change the minutes to a fraction of an hour. That's $\frac{40}{60}$, or $\frac{2}{3}$ of an hour.

$$18 \times \frac{2}{3} = 12$$

So the rider goes 12 miles.

Try this one. A train travels 150 miles in 2 hours and 30 minutes. What's its speed?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

Thirty minutes is half of an hour, so the time is 2.5 hours. The speed is $150 \div 2.5 = 60$ miles per hour.

</details>

## Example 2: The average speed trap

Back to the opening puzzle. Let's work out the totals.

- The trip there takes $60 \div 60 = 1$ hour.
- The trip back takes $60 \div 30 = 2$ hours.
- In total, that's 120 miles in 3 hours.

$$120 \div 3 = 40$$

The average speed is 40 miles per hour, not 45. **Why?** You spend twice as long driving at the slower speed, so the slow part counts for more in the average. Think of it this way: if you drove at 1 mile per hour on the way back, you'd spend 60 hours on that part of the trip, and your average would be close to 2 mph, not 30.

## Example 3: Moving toward each other

Two towns are 210 miles apart. A car leaves one town at 40 miles per hour, and at the same time, a truck leaves the other at 30 miles per hour, heading toward the car. How long until they meet?

**What's a smaller version?** Think about one hour. In that hour, the car covers 40 miles and the truck covers 30 miles, so the gap shrinks by 70 miles.

> **Tip:** When two things move **toward** each other, add the speeds. When one **chases** the other, subtract them.

So the time is $210 \div 70 = 3$ hours.

**Check:** The car goes $40 \times 3 = 120$ miles, and the truck goes $30 \times 3 = 90$ miles. Those add up to 210.

## Example 4: Catching up

Jon starts walking at 3 miles per hour. Two hours later, Mei starts on the same path at 5 miles per hour. How long after Mei starts does she catch him?

**What do we know?** Jon has a head start of $3 \times 2 = 6$ miles.

**What's the plan?** Mei gains on him at $5 - 3 = 2$ miles per hour. To close a 6-mile gap at 2 miles per hour takes $6 \div 2 = 3$ hours.

**Using an equation:** Let $t$ be Mei's walking time. Jon's time is $t + 2$. They've walked the same distance when she catches him:

$$5t = 3(t + 2)$$

This gives $5t = 3t + 6$, so $2t = 6$ and $t = 3$. Both methods agree.

## Example 5: A contest-style question

A boat travels 24 miles upstream and 24 miles back downstream on a river. The boat's speed in still water is 10 miles per hour, and the river flows at 2 miles per hour. How long does the whole trip take?

**What's the question really asking?** Going upstream, the river slows the boat, so its speed is $10 - 2 = 8$ miles per hour. Going downstream, the river helps, so its speed is $10 + 2 = 12$ miles per hour.

- Upstream takes $24 \div 8 = 3$ hours.
- Downstream takes $24 \div 12 = 2$ hours.

The total is 5 hours. By the way, the average speed for the trip is $48 \div 5 = 9.6$ miles per hour, which is below the boat's still-water speed of 10. That's the trap from Example 2 again.

## Going deeper

When an object has length, you have to ask what distance it really covers.

**A harder one:** A train passes a pole in 10 seconds. It crosses a 200-meter bridge in 20 seconds. How long is the train, and how fast is it?

**What distance does it cover?** To pass a pole, the train travels its own length, $L$. To cross a bridge, the front has to cross the bridge, and then the rest of the train has to clear it. So it travels $L + 200$.

**What stays the same?** The speed. Set the two speeds equal:

$\frac{L}{10} = \frac{L + 200}{20}$

Multiply both sides by 20 to get $2L = L + 200$. So $L = 200$ meters. The speed is $200 \div 10 = 20$ meters per second.

**A quick way to see it:** The extra 10 seconds covers exactly the 200-meter bridge. So the speed is 20 meters per second, and the train is as long as 10 seconds of travel.

Try this. A train passes a pole in 8 seconds, and goes through a 300-meter tunnel in 20 seconds. How fast is it?

<details class="check"><summary>Show the answer</summary>

The extra 12 seconds cover the 300-meter tunnel, so the speed is $300 \div 12 = 25$ meters per second.

</details>

<section data-box="traps">

## Common traps

**Averaging the speeds.** The average of 60 and 30 is 45, but the trip's average speed is 40. Speeds can only be averaged directly if you spend the **same time** at each speed, not the same distance.

**Mixing units.** Saying that 18 miles per hour for 40 minutes is $18 \times 40 = 720$ miles gives a silly answer. Convert first, and ask whether the answer is reasonable.

**Treating 30 minutes as 0.3 hours.** There are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100. So 30 minutes is 0.5 hours, and 15 minutes is 0.25 hours.

</section>

<section data-box="strategy">

## Contest strategy

**How to spot it:** Look for "miles per hour," "speed," "how long," or "meet."

**Set up a small table:** Use a row for each part of the trip, and columns for distance, speed, and time. Fill in what you know, and the formula gives you the rest.

**Eliminate:** The average speed has to be between the slowest and fastest speeds. And on a round trip, it's always below the halfway point between them. That can knock out the choice with the simple average.

**Time:** Two minutes.

<section data-box="summary">

## Remember this

- Distance = speed × time.
- Average speed = total distance ÷ total time.
- Keep the units matched, and convert minutes to hours.
- Toward each other: add the speeds.
- Chasing: subtract the speeds.
